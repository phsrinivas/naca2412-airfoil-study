Why this project

A 1930s wing on today's trainer

The Cessna 172 is the airplane a lot of pilots learn on, and it's the most-built aircraft ever. What surprised me is that its wing still uses the NACA 2412 airfoil, a shape designed in the 1930s from a simple formula, not by modern computer optimization.

That made me wonder: if I changed its camber or thickness, could the same airplane fly more efficiently? A new wing shape doesn't need new materials or new parts, just a different template, so it seemed like a realistic thing to test.

What I did

76 tests in NASA FoilSim

I ran 64 tests that covered every combination of four cambers, four thicknesses, and four angles of attack. Then I took the three best shapes and flight-tested them against the stock wing at climb, cruise, and approach.

I cross-checked my numbers against published XFOIL data and University of Illinois wind-tunnel data, and compared my findings with recent studies on the Cessna's wing.

What I learned

"Best" depends on when you ask

  • One test can fool you. Two shapes that beat the stock wing in my first round lost in every real flight condition.
  • Camber matters more than thickness. How curved the wing is drove most of the differences.
  • Old doesn't mean bad. The 1930s design turned out to be a well-balanced compromise for a training airplane.
  • Engineering is tradeoffs. The only improvement I found helped at cruise but hurt at takeoff and landing.
What's next

Questions I still have

I didn't measure stall behavior, and for student pilots, a wing that stalls gently matters as much as one that's efficient. Next I'd like to calculate stall speed directly, test more shapes with finer angle steps, and see how a full 3D wing changes the picture.
