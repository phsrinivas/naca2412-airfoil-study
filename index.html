Can you beat a 90-year-old wing?

The Cessna 172 is the most-built airplane in history, and it still flies on the NACA 2412, a wing shape from the 1930s. I reshaped it 16 different ways in NASA's FoilSim to find something better. Now you can try too.

The question

The NACA 2412 was never optimized the way modern wings are. It came from a simple 1930s formula and was built to be thick, strong, and gentle in a stall. So could a slightly different camber or thickness make the Cessna 172 more efficient, without new materials or parts?

How I tested it

Round 1: 4 cambers × 4 thicknesses × 4 angles of attack = 64 tests at 135 mph and sea level. The three best shapes (NACA 2408, 5408, 5412) all scored highest at 0°.

Round 2: those three plus the stock wing, each tested at climb (85 mph, 10°), cruise (138 mph, 5,000 ft, 3°), and approach (70 mph, 8°).

What I found

Two of the three "winners" lost under every real flight condition. Only NACA 2408 beat the stock wing, and only at cruise. Camber mattered more than thickness, and a result at one test condition didn't predict the rest of the flight. The stock wing turns out to be a pretty well-balanced compromise for a trainer.

Limits and next steps

FoilSim is a simplified simulator, I only tested four values of each variable, and I didn't measure stall behavior, which matters a lot for student pilots. Next I'd test finer angle steps, more shapes, and calculate stall speed directly.

The physics, in four lines
L = ½ ρ V² A CL

Lift: the wing turns air down, the air pushes the wing up.

D = ½ ρ V² A CD

Drag: friction plus pressure, plus swirl at the wingtips.

L / D = CL / CD

Divide one by the other and everything cancels except the shape.

Re ≈ 3.4M – 6.5M

The Reynolds number changes with speed, which is why one test isn't enough.
