01 · Abstract

The question

The Cessna 172, the most produced aircraft in history and a standard flight trainer, still uses a modified NACA 2412 airfoil developed in the 1930s. This study examined whether changing the airfoil's camber or thickness could improve its lift-to-drag ratio (L/D) across the flight conditions a training aircraft uses.

Lift and drag data were generated in NASA's FoilSim simulator in two rounds. Round 1 ran 64 tests that combined four camber values, four thickness values, and four angles of attack at a fixed 135 mph and sea level. Three shapes beat the baseline: NACA 2408, NACA 5408, and NACA 5412. Round 2 tested those three and the baseline under climb, cruise, and approach conditions.

Only NACA 2408 outperformed the baseline, and only at cruise. The two higher-camber shapes were the worst performers under every condition. Good performance at one test condition did not carry over to the rest of the flight envelope, and the modifications tested trade one phase of flight against another instead of improving on the NACA 2412 overall.

Why this matters

The NACA 2412 was never optimized the way modern airfoils are. It came from a general geometric formula, its airflow turns turbulent relatively early, and it was built to be thick, strong, and gentle in a stall.

Recent studies disagree on whether a different airfoil would serve the 172 better. One found the NACA 63-412 beats it at low angles of attack but falls behind at high ones. The real question is whether a trainer should favor efficiency or forgiving handling.

Unlike wingtip devices or composite materials, changing the 2D airfoil shape adds no manufacturing cost once the new shape has been designed and approved.

02 · Background

Reading an airfoil name

In the NACA 4-digit system, the first digit is maximum camber (% of chord), the second is where that camber peaks (tenths of chord), and the last two are maximum thickness (% of chord). Drag the sliders to reshape the wing section.

Lift
L = ½ ρ V² A CL

A wing turns the airflow downward, and by Newton's third law the air pushes the wing up.

Drag
D = ½ ρ V² A CD

Skin friction and pressure resist motion. Induced drag comes from wingtip vortices.

Efficiency
L / D = CL / CD

The shared ½ρV²A terms cancel. A higher L/D means more lift per pound of drag, so the airplane glides farther and burns less fuel.

Reynolds number
Re ≈ 3.4M – 6.5M

Speed and size combined. An airfoil's best L/D changes with Re, so one test condition can't tell the whole story.

03 · Methodology

Two rounds of FoilSim testing

Each test set camber, thickness, and angle of attack in FoilSim's Shape panel, and speed and altitude in its Flight Test panel. Lift, drag, CL, CD, and Reynolds number were recorded, and L/D = lift ÷ drag. Every result was checked against its inputs and for physical plausibility, then cross-checked against published XFOIL data from airfoiltools.com and UIUC wind-tunnel data.

What the data says
  • Camber dominates. 5408 and 5412 perform almost identically despite different thickness.
  • One condition misleads. Two shapes that beat the baseline in Round 1 lost everywhere in Round 2.
  • The 1930s numbers hold up. 2% camber produced the highest L/D anywhere in the 64-test grid.
  • Consistent with prior work. The NACA 63-412 study found the same kind of angle-dependent tradeoff.

06 · Conclusion

A tradeoff, not an upgrade

Three shapes beat the NACA 2412 under a single fixed test condition. Tested across climb, cruise, and approach, only NACA 2408 kept any advantage, and only at cruise. NACA 5408 and NACA 5412 fell behind the baseline everywhere, despite having beaten it in Round 1.

Camber, more than thickness, separated the shapes. NACA 2408's roughly 4.7% cruise gain came at the cost of climb and approach, the two lower-speed phases most relevant to flight training. Like the earlier NACA 63-412 comparison, this suggests the NACA 2412's original design already strikes a reasonable balance for the 172's mission.

Limitations

FoilSim is a simplified educational simulator. Only four values each of camber, thickness, and angle of attack were tested, and the position of maximum camber couldn't be changed in FoilSim. Stall angle and stall speed weren't measured, so the handling and safety question remains open.

Future work

Future work could test a wider range of camber and thickness combinations and use finer angle-of-attack steps to pinpoint each shape's peak L/D. Calculating stall speed and post-stall behavior directly would show how each shape affects the forgiving handling a trainer needs.
